Nineteen sixty-nine Fleet Street is the heart of the publishing industry in the United Kingdom, and also worldwide. Billions of newspapers get printed there every year. The Mirror is the largest such publication, but that’s about to change. Australian businessman and media mogul Rupert Murdoch (played by his countryman Guy Pearce) acquires fast-declining The Sun and he is determined to turn its fortunes around. So he hires ruthless visionary, former Daily Mirror writer and northern editor of the Daily Mail Larry Lamb (Jack O’Connell) as the editor, and young and ambitious Joyce Hopkirk (Claire Foy) as women’s editor. Other recruits include a string of professionals that previously worked their biggest competitors.

The movie begins with a heated exchange between the two male protagonists at Rules, London’s oldest restaurant. They both have n impetuous and forceful personalities, consistently confronting and challenging each other. Murdoch claims that the British need his “antipodean” business acumen (in reference to Australia being at the other end of the planet) and taste for innovation – despite often frowning upon them. It is thanks to the charged chemistry and questionable work ethics (or lack thereof) of these two males that the business begins to grow almost instantly, overtaking the Daily Mail. Their essential premise is very simple: newspaper readers seek entertainment, not information. Veracity becomes thus secondary. They presumably got something right: the circulation of The Sun went from 650,000 to 2,5 million copies in just a few months, eventually becoming the largest of any newspaper in the world.

While Murdoch is the most recognisable real-life character, most of the story evolves around lesser-known Lamb. His ambitions are so unbridled that you can just about make out a spark of madness in his eyes. O’Connell delivers an energetic performance as a man of few morals and principles. The newspaper owner is portrayed as slightly more grounded and responsible (Murdoch reprimands Lamb for the first fully naked girl on print), if still driven by financial interests. These men have little interest in the truth. It is the sales figures that drive their careers and the film forward. A wall chart exhibiting the Sun’s soaring numbers is repeatedly featured throughout the movie.

During the course of 116-minute blend of historical drama and thriller, 69-year-old director Danny Boyle resorts to all sorts of tricks and gimmicks in order to keep audiences hooked. Black-and-white animation alluding to old-fashioned print, large pop-up slogans, fast editing and abundant music provide Ink with a juvenile energy. Wacky allegorical sequences add a touch of absurdity to the proceedings: a journalist covered in feathers and liquid tar is heckled on the streets, while a young Margaret Thatcher tells a frightened Lamb hiding under the table to “rise”.

It’s almost as if Danny Boyle remade Trainspotting (1996) by replacing heroin with adrenaline-inducing power trips. Even the dirty toilet cubicle overhead shots are there, with drug use et al. Unfortunately, the soundtrack is vastly inferior to the classic’s. A couple of punk tunes attempt to inject a touch rebellion into the story, but they get drowned out by the well-worn visuals and trite storytelling devices. This is not a movie that embraces the spirit of punk. There is no affront to the establishment here. On the contrary, Ink is a very conventional movie, viewed from the perspective of the men who control the system.

Much like The Sun does it its readers, Danny Boyle’s 15th feature film underestimates the intelligence of its viewers with cheap thrills and very little substance. The script – penned by screenwriter James Graham – seems to suggest that the British newspaper was the first publication to insert television listings, horoscope and back page sports into its editions. This is not true: all of these practices predate the Sun overhaul by several decades. The newspaper’s real claim to fame was Page 3 girls, a semi-pornographic device that became synonymous with tabloid papers for nearly half a century. The kidnapping of Muriel McKay (Lucy Russell), the wife of Murdoch’s deputy, is introduced in a clunky flash forward in the beginning at the story, and eventually illustrated to inconclusive results. Overall, the plot is overwritten and unbalanced.

This is a movie intended to denounce the demise of journalistic standards, and the advent of the unabashed tabloid culture in the full splendour of vulgarity. Sadly, it does precisely the opposite. The verbose dialogues, the energetic music and frenetic editing create a hypnotic viewing experience, and allegiance with these callous creatures. While Murdoch and Lamb are not likeable as human beings, their achievements are enviable. Perhaps unintentionally and/or overconfident in its abilities to examine history, Ink celebrates the dirty tactics of yellow journalism.

A montage seeks to redeem the movie in the final minutes, revealing the toxic repercussions of tabloid reporting: The Sun’s most reprehensible headlines (the Hillsborough disaster, “Cocaine Kate”, “Freddie Starr ate my hamster”, and so on) are followed by images of controversial people (Andrew Tate, Elon Musk, Bonnie Blue, and many others). No context is provided, and the personalities are barely connected. As a result, criticism is rushed, muddled and vague.

Ink just opened the 83rd Venice Film Festival, and it’s in the event’s Official Competition (vying for the much-coveted Golden Lion).
