Tool's Maynard James Keenan is about to open a sushi restaurant and the menu includes at least one reference to his music career.

Keenan's Queen B Sushi is currently on track to open Sept. 4 in Cottonwood, Arizona.

Reference to Band on Queen B Sushi Menu

The menu on Queen B Sushi's website features a variety of sushi, including rolls, sashimi and nigiri. Names like "spicy tuna," "California" and "shrimp tempura" will look familiar to most anyone who has ordered sushi just about anywhere in the U.S.

But one menu item stands out as a nod to Keenan's career in music and his band, Puscifer.

Diners will find a dish called Puscifer Pops under the "signature rolls" section of the Queen B Sushi menu. The roll combines bluefin and whitefish along with crab, shrimp and ponzu, a citrus-based sauce commonly used in Japanese cuisine.

Full Queen B Sushi Menu Preview

The menu for Keenan's foray into the sushi business is pretty stacked as far as options. Diners can start things off with a series of small plates and appetizers, including asari-clams sakemushi (clams steamed in sake).

Here's a look at Queen B's starters and shared plates:

The sushi menu at Queen B is split into three sections. The signature list offers rolls that contain a more complex list of ingredients.

The Torched Salmon roll, for example, contains salmon, cucumber, avocado and green onion. On top is flame-seared salmon and caviar. Check out the rest of the signature roll list below.

If you're a little less adventurous when eating sushi, the classic rolls menu might be more your speed.

The final section of Queen B's sushi menu is dedicated to sashimi and nigiri.

Why Maynard James Keenan Is Opening a Sushi Restaurant

Queen B Sushi is the latest in Keenan's growing number of businesses outside of the music industry.

The restaurant sits just around the corner from Queen B Vinyl Cafe, which serves as a cafe, music store and even a barbershop known as the Barbifer. Inside the cafe, you can grab a cup of Cafe Puscifer Queen B Coffee or something off the food menu that was "carefully constructed by Maynard James Keenan along with award-winning chef Brent Vibber."

Keenan and his wife, Jen, are also involved in winemaking in the region with their Caduceous Cellars and Merkin Vineyards.

According to the Queen B Sushi website, it was a tour of Japan in the early 2000s performing with A Perfect Circle that made the 62-year-old singer have a deeper interest in sushi.

"It was an eye-opening and mind-blowing experience. While there, I picked up these amazing titanium and hardwood travel chopsticks from Snow Peak. And I've carried them ever since."

Queen B Sushi will be open Thursdays through Monday starting Sept. 4. The restaurant is currently accepting reservations through its website.

So, does getting sushi at Keenan's restaurant make you a top Tool fan? Here are some other factors to consider...

10 Signs You're a Fan of Tool

Gallery Credit: Jordan Blum

CreativaImages, Getty Images/iStockphoto

You’re a Math Nerd

One of Tool’s major trademarks (especially in regard to their last three albums) is the ways in which they integrate numbers into their lyrics, arrangements and artwork. For example, you’ve no doubt noticed that 2001’s Lateralus incorporates the Fibonacci sequence found in nature and elsewhere. Among other connections, the title track’s time signatures – 9/8, 8/8 and 7/8 – correspond to the 16th integer of the sequence: 987. You’ve also noted that the name of 2006’s 10,000 Days relates to either Saturn revolving around the sun or vocalist Maynard James Keenan’s mother, while 2019’s Fear Inoculum centers on the theme of seven.

Vagengeym_Elena, Getty Images/iStockphoto

College Was All About Art Classes

You find that the foursome’s imagery – frequently handled by guitarist Adam Jones and mystical artist Alex Grey – is as emblematic and treasurable as the music it represents. Specifically, the covers and liner notes of both 1993’s Undertow and 1996’s Ænima – as well as 1992’s Opiate EP – are ripe with risqué industrial experimentation. In contrast, the band’s subsequent three records veer toward totally trippy patterns and other iconographic elements, including stereoscopic designs for 10,000 Days. Naturally, Tool expand upon these pictures in concert, too, ensuring that there’s as much to study and appreciate about what you see as what you hear.

fotocelia, Getty Images/iStockphoto

You Grew Up Watching Cronenberg, Lynch and Full Moon Features

Along the same lines, you were raised on terrifying cinema and adore the macabre aspects of Tool’s music videos. Perhaps you developed a childhood proclivity for the Puppet Master series, so you favor the cryptic stop motion ghastliness of “Sober” and “Prison Sex.” Or, maybe the blue-tinged intangible frights of “Stinkfist,” “Ænema” and “Schism” fulfill your need for Cronenberg-esque body horror and Lynchian metaphors and monsters. There’s even the Kafkaesque metamorphoses inherent to the sci-fi “Vicarious,” not to mention the tinges of German Expressionism within “Parabola.” Honestly, every Tool video must tickle your grisly fancy for one reason or another.

Patti Ouderkirk, Getty Images

You Dig Troll/Ironic/Dark Humor

Yes, you enjoy Tool’s critical thinking, but you also welcome their more sarcastic and sophomoric side. For instance, the tongue-in-cheek drug allusions of “Rosetta Stoned” and Lateralus’s “Mantra” consisting of slowed-down sounds of Keenan squeezing his cats. Obviously, “The Gaping Lotus Experience” is an appealing spoken-word jam during which Keenan rambles about “a friend [who] once took some acid / Now he thinks he’s a fire engine,” and Ænima’s “Useful Idiot” uses the static of a needle skipping to fool vinyl listeners. (Closer “Third Eye” begins with a sample from late comedian – and friend of the band – Bill Hicks, too.)

Olivier Le Moal, Getty Images/iStockphoto

There’s No Such Thing as an Overly Lengthy Album

As a fan of Tool – and probably progressive music in general – you value getting the most bang for your buck when an LP lasts as long as possible. Thus, you’re grateful for the fact that all five of the quartet’s main studio collections go for roughly double the duration of a typical record from the mid-1980s and earlier (you know, before the advent of CDs). In particular, the relatively concise Undertow clocks in at just over 69 minutes, whereas Ænima reaches 77 minutes, Lateralus almost hits 79 minutes, 10,000 Days borders on 76 minutes and Fear Inoculum exceeds 79 minutes.

monsitj, Getty Images/iStockphoto

Weird Interludes/Sounds/Recordings Are Right Up Your Alley

You agree that a big part of what makes Tool unique is their use of bizarre voiceovers, sound collages, musical intervals and other segues to enhance the scope of their work. Choice ones include the sheep and crickets within the ominous “Disgustipated;” the carnivalistic playfulness of “Intermission;” the dissonant call about Area 51 on “Faaip de Oiad” (which is Enochian for “Voice of God”); the tribal chanting of “Lipan Conjuring;” and the blend of otherworldly synths and incomprehensible dialogue that peppers “Legion Inoculant.” Rather than view these segments as wasteful, you find that they’re indispensable pieces of Tool’s larger mythos.

Deagreez, Getty Images/iStockphoto

You’re a Connoisseur of the Finer Things in Life

Tool audiences tend to have refined tastes. More likely than not, you – like Keenan – are a wine aficionado whose polished palate, insider knowledge and dedication to superior craftsmanship are key components of your identity and day-to-day life. (Hemispheres’s Shane Clarke notes that Keenan is “a champion of local viticulture” in Arizona, with several vineyards to his name after having been at it for over 20 years.) Likewise, you probably share bassist Justin Chancellor’s love for literature, perhaps even to the point of running your own store as he and wife Shelee did from 2003-2008 (Lobal Orning in Topanga, California).

Island/Atalntic

You’ve Encountered the Crimson King

The eclectic foursome have a wide array of musical influences, but as any diehard devotee knows, arguably their most prevalent and transparent stimuli come from English progressive rock pioneers King Crimson. Both Keenan and Jones have cited the band as a major motivator (especially founding guitarist and mastermind Robert Fripp), and they even toured together in 2001. Whether you were already a follower of Fripp and company or you discovered them around the turn of the century (hell, “Schism” feels like a lost track from Red), you pretty much have to rock with the Crimson King to rock with Tool.

gustavofrazao, Getty Images/iStockphoto

Philanthropy Is Totally Metal

You appreciate that Keenan’s philanthropic endeavors reveal how the metal community is often inclusive and altruistic. For example, he appeared at 2004’s Axis of Justice music festival (which was organized by Serj Tankian and Tom Morello to “bring together musicians, fans of music, and grassroots political organizations to fight for social justice”). The following year, he appeared with Alice in Chains at a Seattle benefit concert for the victims of the 2004 Indian Ocean earthquake and tsunami disaster. Plus, he auctioned off a couple of platinum plaques in 2013 to help fund the recovery of Oklahoma’s tornado-damaged Animal Resource Center.

araelf, Getty Images/iStockphoto

No Subject Matter is Too Perverse or Philosophical

You venerate the group’s knack for mixing the taboo with the thought-provoking, such as how the controversial “Prison Sex” holds a deeper message about “recognizing” and “work[ing] through” child abuse. Similarly, “Stinkfist” is about fearlessness and “grabb[ing] life by the throat,” whereas “Hooker with a Penis” confronts the notion of artistic commodification and “selling out.” Of course, their songs have become less overtly racy and more metaphysical since then. Case in point: the exploration of mortality and redemption seen in the two-part “Wings for Marie,” as well as Fear Inoculum’s overarching commentary on “getting older and more comfortable with yourself.”
